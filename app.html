      Receipt

      Remittance (stub / coupon)

      The tear-off portion you submit along with payment so the creditor can match money to the right account. It carries your account number, the amount due, and the creditor's payment address / bank encoding. On its own it is an instruction slip — it is not money, and sending the stub alone does not pay anything.

      1 · You payYou hand/send the remittance with actual payment — cash, cheque, debit, or transfer — to your financial institution (your FI).
      2 · Your FI exchanges itYour FI processes the stub as a credit item representing value — value that came from your payment — into the clearing system.
      3 · Creditor's FI receives itBank-to-bank clearing between financial institutions settles the value, FI to FI.
      4 · Creditor is creditedThe corporate creditor gets the funds and posts them to your account number from the stub. No stub + no money = no credit.

      What Rule H6 itself says — so you can compare

      You will hear that a remittance coupon is payment by itself, without money leaving any account. Here is what the rule says, so you can judge that claim against the source: in Rule H6 the remittance is submitted along with payment and becomes a credit item when exchanged by the payor's FI — inside bank-to-bank clearing, backed by the payment submitted with it. In the rule's own words, the stub travels with the payment. What you do with that comparison is up to you — see the checked demo below for the full text.

      What the video claims

      That a $0 entry is a legal way to pay bills: monthly bills are “remittances” under Canada's Payments Rule H6, the U.S. definition of “payment” covers a noncash delivery that liquidates an unmatured obligation (a mortgage), and the United States and Canada are “under the same footing” under the Articles of Confederation — so a $0 entry qualifies as payment for utility bills, medical bills, and mortgages.

      1 · Rule H6 — “Remittance” Quote VERIFIED $0 reading CONTRADICTED

      “…submitted to the Payor's FI by the Payor along with payment, and which itself becomes a credit item representing value when subsequently Exchanged by the Payor's FI”Payments Canada, Rule H6 — definition of “Remittance”

      The same rule defines Remittance Amount as the monetary amount submitted in payment of an Invoice.

      What it does NOT say
      It does not say the stub pays by itself. The remittance travels along with payment, and the amount submitted is the amount paid. A $0 entry submits a $0 amount.
      Verdict
      $0 is a $0 amount — “along with payment” is required. The stub routes the payment; it does not replace it.

      2 · 12 U.S.C. § 4402(15) — “payment” Quote VERIFIED $0 discharge assembly CONTRADICTED

      “The term 'payment' means a payment of United States dollars, another currency, or a composite currency, and a noncash delivery, including a payment or delivery to liquidate an unmatured obligation.”12 U.S.C. § 4402(15)

      Scope line, from the same subsection: “For purposes of this subchapter” — Chapter 45, Payment System Risk Reduction: netting between financial institutions, not consumer bill payment.

      What it does NOT say
      It does not define “payment” for your mortgage, utility, or medical bill. Inside its own subchapter, “noncash delivery” is about financial institutions settling obligations with each other — not a customer sending $0 to a servicer.
      Verdict
      Quote VERIFIED, $0 discharge assembly CONTRADICTED.

      3 · Articles of Confederation, Article XI — Canada Quote VERIFIED Same-footing claim CONTRADICTED

      “Canada acceding to this confederation, and joining in the measures of the united states, shall be admitted into, and entitled to all the advantages of this union”Articles of Confederation, Article XI

      Context: the Articles were effective 1781 to 1789 and were replaced by the U.S. Constitution. The offer was conditional — if Canada acceded. Canada never acceded.

      What it does NOT say
      It does not put the United States and Canada under the same footing today, and it does not make a Canadian bank-clearing rule govern a U.S. bill.
      Verdict
      Quote VERIFIED, US/Canada same-footing claim CONTRADICTED.
      Overall: all three quotes are real — VERIFIED. Assembled into “a $0 entry pays your bills,” the claim is CONTRADICTED by the full text of the same sources: payment must be submitted along with the remittance, § 4402(15) is scoped to bank netting, and the Articles of Confederation no longer govern either country.

      1 · UCC 2022 Amendments — the app's UCC readings, checked against current text Checked

      The library holds the Uniform Commercial Code 2022 Amendments — Final Act with Comments (Uniform Law Commission / ALI). Those amendments mainly do three things: they add new Article 12 for “controllable electronic records” (certain digital assets), they update Articles 1, 2, 2A, 3, 4, 4A, 5, 7, 8 and 9 for electronic records and signatures, and they set transition rules. States adopt the UCC state by state, so which version governs you depends on your state's enacted text — check your state code for the version in force there.

      What this check found
      The provisions this app quotes in its demos — Article 7 documents of title, Article 8 securities entitlements, and the Article 3 / Article 4 payment language — were not rewritten in substance by the 2022 amendments. The amendments modernize how those articles handle electronic records; they do not add a birth-certificate, trust-account, or bill-discharge provision to any article. No demo in this app needed its verdict changed on that ground.
      What it does NOT say
      The 2022 Act does not say the older UCC in the library (about 2003 vintage) is “fake,” and it does not say every state has adopted the 2022 text. Both overstatements would be wrong. Use the 2022 Final Act for current uniform text, and your own state's code for the law where you are.

      2 · Is a birth certificate a warehouse receipt? — Deschesne (2005), tested against UCC § 7-202 Claim NOT ESTABLISHED

      The library piece — Deschesne, “Structure of the Birth Certificate” (2005) — argues a birth certificate matches the form of a warehouse receipt under UCC § 7-202. Here is that test, requirement by requirement. § 7-202(a) says a warehouse receipt need not be in any particular form; § 7-202(b) lists what a warehouse must include to avoid liability for omission — including the warehouse's location, the receipt number, a statement the goods will be delivered to bearer / to the order of a named person / to a named person or assigns, storage rates, and a description of the goods or packages.

      § 7-202 elementBirth certificateLabel
      Identifies a person and carries a record numberYes — name, date, certificate / file number. This surface resemblance is real.Holds — as resemblance
      Issued by a warehouse for goods received for storageNo warehouseman is named, no goods are described as received for storage, and no storage terms appear. The issuer is a vital-records registrar.Not established
      States goods will be delivered to bearer / order of a named personNo delivery promise of that kind appears on a birth certificate.Not established
      Storage / handling rates, signature of the warehouse or its agent (§ 7-202(b))Not present in that form.Not established
      Plain reading
      Where it holds: both are numbered government / commercial records that identify someone. Where it breaks: § 7-202 is about a warehouse holding goods. A document is not a warehouse receipt because it has a name and a number — the warehouse, the goods, and the delivery promise are the substance of the section, and none of the three was found on a birth certificate. That is what the statute says. Whether you still want to raise the argument somewhere is your call — now you can see exactly which lines it has to answer.

      3 · What did the word mean? — Black's 1st (1891) vs. 2nd (1910) + Skeat Reference

      Source“Person”

      4 · Trust words & maxims — real terms for Trusts mode Reference

      From the library's Trust Glossary and Maxims of Law. Use these when you read any trust — including your own document in Trusts mode above:

      Settlor / grantor · Trustee · Beneficiary · Cestui que trust
      The settlor creates the trust and puts property in. The trustee holds legal title and must manage it for someone else. The beneficiary — in older equity language, the cestui que trust — holds the equitable / beneficial interest. Three roles, three different rights. Having an interest is not the same as having authority to administer.
      Res / corpus · Fiduciary
      The res (or corpus) is the actual property in the trust — no property transferred in, no funded trust, whatever the paper is called. A fiduciary must act for the beneficiary's benefit, not their own; that duty is what courts enforce.
      Maxims — what they actually are
      Maxims are short statements of legal principles, not statutes. Real examples: “Equity follows the law” (equity supplements the law, it does not repeal it) and “He who seeks equity must do equity” (a court of equity can require fairness from the person asking). A maxim states a principle a court may apply — quoting one does not by itself decide a case, and no maxim in the collection creates an account or a payment right.

      5 · How money & shares actually move — FedNow + DTC System facts

      For bills and brokerage statements, the library holds the operators' own rulebooks: the FedNow Service Operating Procedures (with Federal Reserve Operating Circular 8) and the DTC Operational Arrangements. What they describe is plumbing — stated here as facts about the systems, so you can compare any claim against them:

      FedNow — payments
      FedNow is the Federal Reserve's instant-payment service (live since July 2023). Participating banks and credit unions send and receive payments in seconds, 24/7, with immediate final settlement in Federal Reserve accounts. A payment moves value from the sender's account to the receiver's account through their institutions — the message and the money travel together. There is no step in the operating procedures where a coupon, endorsement, or $0 instruction settles a payment by itself.
      DTC — securities (the indirect holding system)
      Most U.S. shares are not held as paper certificates in your name. Under the indirect holding system (UCC Article 8), the Depository Trust Company holds them through its nominee, Cede & Co., your broker holds an entitlement against DTC, and you hold a security entitlement against your broker. Your brokerage statement is evidence of that entitlement — it is not itself a certificate, a warehouse receipt, or a direct claim on the issuer. That structure is real, it is documented in DTC's own arrangements, and it is exactly why a statement and a certificate are different papers.
      Why this matters for your paper
      When a bill or statement confuses you, ask which system it runs on: a card network, ACH, FedNow, or DTC book-entry. The answer is usually printed on the document — and the Who / Where tool above maps the companies named on yours.

      7 · Note vs. draft — acceptance only exists on a draft Acceptance-on-a-note: application not established

      Every reading in this app now classifies the instrument first, because the most common category error in this space is applying acceptance — a draft concept — to a note. The Code draws the line in one sentence:

      An instrument is a “note” if it is a promise and is a “draft” if it is an order. If an instrument falls within the definition of both “note” and “draft,” a person entitled to enforce the instrument may treat it as either.UCC § 3-104(e) — uniform text
      “Acceptance” means the drawee’s signed agreement to pay a draft as presented. It must be written on the draft and may consist of the drawee’s signature alone.UCC § 3-409(a) — uniform text · § 3-410 covers acceptance that varies the draft
      The rule the reader enforces
      A note is a promise, not an order — there is no drawee to accept it. If a document is classified as a note and the document or the claim pasted with it uses “accepted,” “acceptance,” or “acceptor,” the reading is auto-flagged Application not established with this fixed explanation: “Acceptance is defined in UCC § 3-409 as the drawee’s signed agreement to pay a draft. A note is a promise, not an order. No acceptance occurs on a note under Article 3.” Whenever acceptance language appears at all, the reading forces the visible check: “Acceptance under § 3-409 applies only to drafts. This instrument is classified as: [type].”
      Discharge is party-specific — § 3-601
      Discharge under Article 3 discharges a party; it does not automatically extinguish the instrument against everyone else. The paper can still run against other parties. “Discharged” never means “the debt is gone” without naming which party was discharged.
      The mechanisms, with their limits
      Tender (§ 3-603): refused tender discharges indorsers and accommodation parties to the extent of the amount tendered and stops post-maturity interest on that amount — it does not discharge the principal obligor. Accord and satisfaction (§ 3-311 / Tenn. § 47-3-311): the person against whom the claim is asserted has to prove (i) they tendered the instrument in good faith as full satisfaction, (ii) the amount was unliquidated or subject to a bona fide dispute, and (iii) the claimant got payment — and the claim is discharged only if the instrument or an accompanying writing carried a conspicuous statement that it was tendered as full satisfaction. On an undisputed, liquidated debt it does nothing. Two ways it can still fail under § 47-3-311(c): (c)(1) — the claimant is an organization that had already sent a conspicuous statement telling you to send disputed-debt papers to a designated person, office, or place, and the instrument didn't go there; and (c)(2) — the claimant tenders repayment of the amount within 90 days after payment. There is no “not already certified or returned” condition in § 3-311 — that certified-check wording lives in § 3-602, not here. Limitations (§ 3-118): six years on a note payable at a definite time (from the due date), six years after demand on a demand note (barred after ten continuous years with no payment and no demand), three years after dishonor on an unaccepted draft (or ten years after its date, whichever expires first). Lost, destroyed, or stolen (§ 3-309): the person seeking enforcement must show they were entitled to enforce when the loss occurred (or acquired ownership from someone who was), that the loss was not the result of a transfer by them or lawful seizure, and that they cannot reasonably obtain possession — and the court must ensure the payor is adequately protected against double liability.
      Discharge by payment — Tennessee is different (§ 47-3-602)
      You may have read this rule: “keep paying the old holder until you get signed notice the note was transferred — then pay the new one.” That protection is real in the 2022 uniform UCC § 3-602(b) text. Tennessee's older § 47-3-602 (Acts 1995, ch. 397, § 2) was the earlier form — the pre-amendment text: payment discharged you only to the extent you paid a person entitled to enforce the instrument (§ 47-3-301). The only exceptions that blocked discharge in that pre-amendment text were (b)(1) — you knew payment was barred by a court injunction, or you took indemnity from someone claiming the instrument — and (b)(2) — you knew the instrument was stolen and paid the person you knew was wrongfully holding it. Under that pre-amendment text, paying a former holder who was no longer a person entitled to enforce did not discharge the note under § 47-3-602(a). That does not by itself decide what happened to any one payment — whether the person you paid was authorized to take it, whether a separate servicing rule applies, and whether the payment was credited are separate questions. HB 1800 / SB 1859 was enacted as Public Chapter 704, effective July 1, 2026, adopting the 2018 and 2022 UCC amendments generally. Whether Public Chapter 704 amended § 47-3-602 itself — and whether the transfer-notice protection is now Tennessee law — is not yet established; a citation-grade check of the chaptered act is still needed.
      Assignment is not negotiation
      Negotiation (§ 3-201) is what makes someone a holder. Transfer (§ 3-203) vests the right to enforce but does not by itself make the transferee a holder, and only a holder (or a person with a holder’s rights) can be a holder in due course (§ 3-302). A pure assignment transfers rights; it does not negotiate the instrument.
      Federal Reserve notes — 12 U.S.C. § 411
      Federal Reserve notes “shall be obligations of the United States” and “shall be redeemed in lawful money on demand at the Treasury Department of the United States… or at any Federal Reserve bank.” After the 1934 amendment removed gold redemption, “lawful money” includes Federal Reserve notes themselves — the redemption right is circular by design. That is the statute’s design, stated plainly, not a hidden flaw.
      FTC Holder Rule — 16 C.F.R. Part 433 (consumer contracts)
      Consumer credit contracts must carry this notice (at least 10-point bold type): “ANY HOLDER OF THIS CONSUMER CREDIT CONTRACT IS SUBJECT TO ALL CLAIMS AND DEFENSES WHICH THE DEBTOR COULD ASSERT AGAINST THE SELLER OF GOODS OR SERVICES OBTAINED PURSUANT HERETO OR WITH THE PROCEEDS HEREOF. RECOVERY HEREUNDER BY THE DEBTOR SHALL NOT EXCEED AMOUNTS PAID BY THE DEBTOR HEREUNDER.” If the notice is missing from a consumer credit contract, the missing notice itself is the violation — no broken-chain theory is required.

      8 · Tennessee interest — there is no single flat cap (Title 47, Chapter 14) Checked Nov. 21, 2026

      People will tell you “Tennessee caps interest at 10%.” That is not how the law is written. Tennessee sets the maximum in tiers, and which tier you land in depends on the kind of deal you signed:

      Signed written contracts — the formula rate (§ 47-14-103(2))
      If you signed a written contract and no special statute fixes a different rate for that kind of loan, the maximum is the formula rate (§§ 47-14-102(7), 47-14-103(2)): 4 points above the average prime rate the Federal Reserve publishes, and never more than 24%. The Commissioner of Financial Institutions publishes the current number. As of November 10, 2026, the published formula rate is 11.00% — 4 points over a 7.00% average prime rate. That number moves when prime moves; check the Commissioner's current announcement for today's figure.
      The 10% tier — what's left over (§ 47-14-103(3))
      The 10% per annum rate in § 47-14-103(3) is only for transactions not otherwise governed — no special statute, and no signed written contract putting them in the formula-rate tier. In practice that is mostly deals without a signed written contract. It is not a cap sitting on top of every loan.
      Small single-payment loans (§ 47-14-104)
      A single-payment loan of $1,000 or less with a term of one year or less has its own rule at § 47-14-104: the maximum is the rate the Commissioner sets by rule as fair and reasonable, never above 10%, plus a limited loan charge set in the section. Read § 47-14-104 itself before you quote a number for a loan like that.
      Where the constitutional 10% fits (Tenn. Const. art. XI, § 7)
      The Tennessee Constitution says the General Assembly shall define and regulate interest — and that if no applicable statute is enacted, the rate shall not exceed 10%. That 10% operates only when the legislature has enacted nothing on the point. The legislature has enacted Chapter 14, so the tiers above are what govern — the constitutional line is the fallback, not a second cap on top of them.
      What this does NOT say
      This entry does not say what rate your contract charges, or whether a charge on your paper was lawful — that turns on the contract you signed, which tier it falls in, and any special statute for that kind of loan. Upload the contract above and the reader will quote the rate language it actually contains, word for word.
